Coven Hollow Build Plans

Construction sheets, added one build at a time. Village style: early Victorian, roughly 3/4 scale, finished on all four sides.

Sheet 1 · Cackle Crust Bakery (rev. C: four-sided, walk-in, collapsible)

Early Victorian gable-front shop, ~3/4 scale · footprint 72″ × 60″ · walls 66″, ridge 84″, chimney pots to ~112″ · a real person stands inside to sell · folds flat on hinges · rated for canyon gusts · ~$180–260 · three weekends

An 1840s village bakery at the Hollow's scale: about three-quarters of full size, finished on all four sides for the people who wander through, and open where every other building is shut. The front gable carries a Victorian shopfront: a serving window where you (or whoever plays baker that night) stand inside at a real counter to sell bread and hand out treats, and a small-paned display window stacked with goods, with a display board of loaves out front. The whole building is four hinged wall panels plus a folding roof: it goes up in twenty minutes, pins together rigid, stakes to the ground against canyon wind, and stores as a flat stack in November.

The 3/4-scale cheat sheet

Theme parks shrink buildings the same way: the dressing shrinks, the functional parts don't. Anything a human touches stays near full size; anything the eye measures goes to 3/4. That's what makes a small building read as charming instead of wrong.

Plan view & fold scheme

Plan and fold scheme. Two corners get ordinary fixed hinges; the two diagonal corners get loose-pin hinges (3 per corner). Pins in: a rigid closed box. Pins out: two L-pairs that fold flat. This becomes the standard system for every four-sided build in the Hollow.

Wind plan (canyon gusts)

Design target: your ~17 mph canyon winds with margin to roughly 30 mph gusts. A closed four-sided box is already the strongest shape in this village; wind pushes on about 35 sq ft of wall and meets a staked, pinned ring of panels instead of a flat sail. The load on the worst wall in a 30 mph gust is on the order of 80 lb, and the anchoring below covers that several times over.

  • Corners pinned full height: 3 hinges per corner (top, middle, bottom) so the box acts as one ring, not four flaps.
  • Four 18″ rebar stakes, one through each corner base plate, driven at a slight inward lean.
  • Roof positively attached: 8 hook-and-eyes (2 per roof edge) latching roof frame to wall plates. Roof lift is the only real failure mode; gravity is not a fastener.
  • Two 30 lb sandbags inside against the side walls (invisible from outside, they double as prop weights).
  • Optional for exposed spots: a ratchet strap over the ridge to two spiral ground anchors, hidden under the ridge cap trim.

The serving space (where you stand)

Inside is a real workspace: standing room at the counter (full height, thanks to the gable front), candy and bread within reach, and set dressing that reads as a baker who just stepped into the back. When no one is staffing it, the space still tells the story:

  • The apron on its hook just inside the opening, strings dangling.
  • A rolling pin set down mid-task with flour dust around it (flour sealed under matte spray).
  • A brass balance scale mid-weigh, one pan holding a paper-wrapped loaf tied with twine.
  • An open ledger with a dip pen across it. One visible entry: “Apothecary. 2 soul cakes. UNPAID.”
  • A brass counter bell and a card: “Ring bell. Or don't. She hears everything anyway.”
  • Interior shelves of foam loaves with one gap where something just sold. A shelf at hip height inside holds the real candy stock, out of sight.

Panel framing

Panel framing. Every panel is a 2x2 frame with foam board skin, light enough for one person to carry, joined at the corners by hinges. The 3/4-scale roof pitch comes from the 18″ rise over the 36″ half-span.

Assembly order

  1. Build six panels flat: two gable panels (front and back), two side walls, two roof panels. 2x2 frames, gussets at every joint, blocking where the drawing shows it.
  2. Skin, carve, paint flat. Foam board on (adhesive + washer screws), clapboard scored with a ballpoint pen, timber paint (dark base, gray dry-brush, cream on the raised grain, dark wash in the scores). Painting flat on sawhorses beats painting standing up in every way.
  3. Shopfront joinery on the front panel: pilasters built out 1″ proud with molding caps, stallriser panels in dark green, cornice, and the gable sign hand-lettered in gilt over guide lines.
  4. Serving window: fixed raised sash with lattice muntins, counter bolted to its blocking at 34″.
  5. Display window: clear plastic behind a nine-pane muntin grid, two shallow shelves behind it, stocked. Flicker bulb low inside.
  6. Staff door hung on the back panel, 26″ clear, painted as a paneled Victorian door.
  7. Hinge the box: stand panels in place, fixed hinges at two corners, loose-pin hinges at the two diagonal corners, 3 per corner. Check the box squares up, then number every hinge leaf with a paint pen for next-year assembly.
  8. Roof: piano hinge at the ridge joins the two panels; foam shingles with 3/4-scale exposure; hook-and-eyes latch it to the wall plates. Ridge cap trim hides the hinge (and the optional strap).
  9. Chimney on the right side wall: form tube on its mast, brick-scored skin, terracotta pots, smoke gap in the cap.
  10. Smoke rig: fog machine in a vented weather box at the chimney base, duct up, fan under the cap, one cycle timer: ten seconds of puff, ninety of rest.
  11. Anchor: four rebar stakes through the corner plates, sandbags inside, hook-and-eyes latched. Shake test at the ridge.
  12. Dress the set: serving space per the list, display board on trestles, Black Cauldron Milling sacks by the staff door, A-frame chalkboard by the path (“Soul cakes 2d. Hot cross buns, blessing included. Day-old spells half off.”), cinnamon broom behind the sacks, interior amber strip on, lantern lit.
Village standards set by this sheet, for every build from here on: roughly 3/4 scale with functional parts kept near full size; four finished sides on anything visitors walk around; the pin-hinge panel system for collapse; corner stakes + positive roof attachment + interior ballast for canyon wind; and early Victorian everywhere: flame-warm light only, hand-painted gilt serif signage, iron and brass hardware, wood, wicker, paper, and cloth containers, prices in pence, and every cord and machine hidden inside a build or weather box. Nothing invented after about 1845 in sight.

Sheet 2 · The Owl Post

Early Victorian post office kiosk, ~3/4 scale · footprint 30″ × 30″ · walls 56″, ridge 78″ · 12 named mailboxes · working letter slot · one weekend · ~$60–100 · night delivery only

The smallest build in the village and the one that does the most narrative work: a wall of pigeonhole mailboxes puts every witch's name in one place, so every sold tag, citation, and ledger entry elsewhere in the Hollow suddenly connects to a resident. Early Victorian post office details throughout: dark green paneling, gilt lettering, brass fittings, and a raven on the ridge with an envelope, since in this village the ravens carry the mail (which is why the ordinance forbids caging them; they're the workforce). Small enough that it doesn't need the fold system: it moves whole, the roof lifts off, and it stores standing up.

Front elevation. The gilt sign, the pigeonhole wall with a name under every box, a brass posting slot that actually works, and the raven postman on the ridge. One cubby glows amber from behind: somebody's got mail.

Side section. The rear panel swings open for restocking and letter collection.   • The brass slot is real: kids post letters to the residents all October.
  • A small placard: “Letters to the residents of Coven Hollow. Replies delivered by raven before next All Hallows.”

Sheet 3 · Bristle & Twig, Broom Wrights

Early Victorian trade workshop, ~3/4 scale · footprint 60″ × 48″ · front wall 66″, mono-pitch roof falling to 50″ · doors stand open, interior on display · pin-hinge collapsible · one to two weekends · ~$100–150 · brooms made, mended & recovered from trees

The workshop that explains your existing broom parking rail: somebody has to service all those brooms. Where the bakery is a shop, this is a trade building, so it gets a workshop's shape: a mono-pitch roof sloping from a tall front to a low back, plank double doors thrown open all evening, and the workbench in plain view with a broom half-bound in the vise. Victorian trade shops hung oversized symbols of their craft over the street (a giant boot, a giant key), so Bristle & Twig hangs a giant broom. Your existing bound grass sheaves finally get their backstory: they're the raw bristle stock, stacked against the wall.

Front elevation

Front elevation. Doors pinned open against the wall, lantern-lit interior on display: tool wall, twine spools, and the workbench with a broom half-bound in the vise, shavings on the floor. The giant broom trade sign hangs over the path; the stock rack sells finished brooms in pence.

Plan & side section

Plan and section. The mono-pitch roof is one panel, making this the easiest collapse in the village. Doors fold flat against the wall and hook there; in a serious wind warning, lift them off their pin hinges entirely and lay them inside.

Set dressing & gags

  • The bench mid-task: a broom clamped in the vise with binding twine still wrapped around it and hanging loose. Real wood shavings on the floor, free from any woodshop.
  • Stock rack outside: four to six finished brooms, paper tags in pence (“4d”, “6d, ash handle”), one tagged SOLD in a witch's name once the cast is christened.
  • The customer lot: your existing parking rail gets a “BRISTLE & TWIG CUSTOMERS ONLY” placard, one broom wears a boot with a citation tied on (“Per Hollow ordinance 7: overnight roosting”).
  • The tow job: a crashed broom wedged in a nearby tree or hedge, tagged “RECOVERY REQUESTED, awaiting ladder.” The most photographed square foot in the yard, guaranteed.
  • The fitting post: a marked measuring post by the door: “Broom fitting: stand here.” Height marks labeled “First broom,” “Learner,” “Licensed,” “Show-off.” Kids measure themselves all night and it costs you one post and a paint pen.
  • Repair queue: three or four battered brooms leaning by the door with paper claim tickets: “bent in a hex,” “cat damage,” “pulls left.”

Sheet 4 · The Lychgate & Fingerpost

Village entrance, early Victorian lychgate style · 48″ opening, posts 78″, roof peak ~92″ · posts drop into ground sleeves (lift out to store) · one weekend · ~$50–80

English villages of this era marked their thresholds with lychgates: a small roofed gate you pass beneath. That's exactly the job here. Crossing under a roof, even a tiny one, tells visitors the rules changed on the other side. The sign facing the street says where they've arrived; the sign on the back, which they only see leaving, says what they're returning to. The fingerpost stands just inside, pointing the way to every resident.

Elevation

The lychgate and fingerpost. Back of the hanging sign reads “NOW RETURNING TO THE MORTAL WORLD. MIND THE GAP BETWEEN REALITIES.” The fingerpost's arrows point at the real buildings, so the joke also works as wayfinding.

Sheet 5 · The Diviner's Tower

Hexagonal observatory tower, ~3/4 scale · 36″ across flats · walls 66″, cone peak ~96″ · accordion-fold collapse · two weekends · ~$110–160 · fortunes, star charts & weather hexes

Every building so far is a tradesman's: timber, green paint, gilt. The Diviner is not a tradesman, and her house says so. A slim six-sided tower in midnight blue scattered with gold stars, a moon dial on the wall, a brass telescope poking through a shutter at the sky, and a curtained doorway lit from within. Early Victorians were mad for astronomy (backyard observatories were a genuine craze), so the tower reads period AND otherworldly. She earns the village's one exception to the flame-light rule: her doorway glows a slow violet, because scrying is in progress, and one cool light in a warm village reads as magic.

The implied population · tiny doors 10″ × 14″ at tree bases · cottage silhouette 40″ × 48″ in the darkest corner · scattered evenings · ~$30–50 · residents you never meet

The cheapest square feet in the whole village, and among the most effective. Tiny doors at the bases of real trees imply residents at a whole other scale, living their own lives underfoot. The distant cottage is a flat black silhouette with one warm lit window, parked in the darkest corner of the yard: from the sidewalk it reads as another street of the Hollow trailing off into the dark. Both work by the same rule: the imagination finishes whatever the light starts.

Details

The tiny door gets real hardware details (strap hinges, a brass bead knob, a GO AWAY mat, its own scarlet mailbox on a twig post). The cottage is matte black plywood or foam with one warm window; darkness does the rest of the construction for free.

Build notes

PartHow
Tiny doors (make 2–3)Scrap 1x material or doubled foam board, arched, plank-scored, with strap hinges cut from black foam and a bead or drawer-pull knob. Porthole window backed with amber film and a micro fairy light (coin battery with timer, swapped weekly). Strap to the tree with a black bungee behind ivy, or lean-stake it; never screw into the tree.
DressingDoormat from a sample scrap lettered in paint pen. A twig-post mailbox, scarlet like the pillar box: the Owl Post delivers at every scale. Optional milk-bottle-cap sized bottles by the door on delivery day.
CottageOne sheet of 1/2″ ply or foam board cut as a gabled silhouette with a chimney, painted the flattest black available. The window opening gets amber film and a flicker bulb in a weatherproof box behind. Two stakes and a kickstand, plus one sandbag: it's a flat, so treat it like a sail and tuck it into the wind shadow of the fence or shrubs.
PlacementThe whole trick is placement: darkest corner, partially masked by real foliage, window angled toward the sidewalk sightline. If a visitor can see its edges, move it deeper. The moment it reads clearly, it stops working.
Scale noteThe cottage is roughly half scale, and that's on purpose. Smaller than the 3/4-scale village implies it's far away: forced perspective doing the work of thirty feet of yard you don't have.
